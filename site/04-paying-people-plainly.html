Chapter 4: Paying People Plainly

Education only, never legal or tax advice. Tax specifics (forms, deadlines, withholding, deductibility) vary and change — this chapter explains the shape of things so your accountant conversation takes ten minutes, not ten hours.

Plain beats clever

Payment isn't a mystery to solve; it's a promise to keep. Plain looks like:

  • an agreed rate, in writing, before work starts;
  • an invoice from them, a payment from you, both timestamped and traceable;
  • a payment date that always happens, with no renegotiating.

That's most of it. Everything else is detail.

Rate vs. flat fee — when each fits

StructureFits whenWatch out for
HourlyWork varies in size; scope fuzzyAmbiguity breeds anxiety on both sides. Ask for honest hour tracking
Flat fee per projectScope is crystal clearScope creep — they do more work than paid for
Flat monthly retainerRecurring, similar volume each monthAgree up front what "inside" the retainer is
Per-unit (per transcript, per episode)Work scales in clean unitsUnits should be easy to count, no judgment calls

Pick the least weird one for the work. For first hires, hourly with a cap ("I'm budgeting ~5 hrs/week; flag me before you exceed it") is often the friendliest: it protects you from runaway totals and protects them from underestimated scope.

The paperwork, in plain language

The contractor side of US small-business hiring, in one breath:

  • W-9: a one-page form the contractor fills out for you (name, address, tax ID type). It's what you need before paying them. No withholding happens here — you're not their employer for tax purposes if they're genuinely independent.
  • Invoice: the contractor sends you one per pay period (a simple template works fine). You pay it, and you keep it. That invoice is your proof that you paid.
  • 1099-NEC: if you pay a contractor $600+ over the year, you generally report that to the IRS at year-end. Your accountant will confirm your specifics; your job in the meantime is just to (a) collect W-9s on day one and (b) track totals. Chapter 5's log makes that effortless.
  • No withholding, no unemployment filing (this is what "independent contractor" usually means in practice) — but only when the working relationship really is independent (Chapter 1). Don't retrofit; start right.

If instead you're hiring a true employee, stop and involve a payroll service or accountant — that's a different, more regulated path and worth doing right rather than improvising.

How payment actually happens

Options, friendliest first for a tiny business:

  • Check-by-hand or ACH push through your bank (slow but fine).
  • A payment processor (Stripe, PayPal, etc.) — simple, small fee, and both sides get a clean timestamped record. Note: platforms have their own tax paperwork flows; read once, then forget.
  • Local bank transfer if you're both American and your bank makes it easy.

Whatever you do: pay them and write down that you paid. Method matters less than traceability.

The rule that matters more than the paperwork

Pay people on the day you said you'd pay them. Every time. Even when you're busy. Even when some earlier invoice felt imperfect.

Why this is the whole chapter in one sentence: cash-flow delays hurt part-time people disproportionately more than they hurt a company, and one late payment can make someone feel taken advantage of. Meanwhile paying early has a bizarre effect: it makes people go the extra mile without being asked. Pay on time, every time, and you've already outperformed most of the business world.
