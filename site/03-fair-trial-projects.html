Chapter 3: Fair Trial Projects

Education only, never legal or tax advice.

Why a paid trial, always

Want to know if someone can do the work? There are exactly two reliable ways:

  1. See work they've already done (portfolio, samples, references).
  2. Pay them a small amount to do a small piece of your work.

The second is often better because your work is specific — your voice, your files, your weird naming convention.

And the critical word is paid. Unpaid trial work means the person spends real time-and-skill on your behalf with no compensation, which in most settings is (a) exploitative, and (b) — take this seriously — a place where "unpaid trial" can shade into illegal unpaid labor, which is exactly the archetype regulators worry most about.

Rule: if it looks like work, it is work. Pay for it. Small budget, real pay, zero exceptions.

The anatomy of a good trial

  • Scope: 2 to 6 hours of real (but not precious) work.
  • Stakes: something you'd be fine redoing yourself.
  • Paid: at your real rate, or a fixed flat fee ("$75 flat").
  • Feedback loop: you'll give feedback once, mid-trial, so you can see how they handle it. (More important than the output quality in many ways!)
  • Time window: a clear deadline (e.g., "due Friday, 4pm") both to respect their time and because responsiveness under a deadline is also signal.
  • No free asset transfer: you're not asking for a finished website you can keep. You're paying for evidence of approach, not for a product.

What a trial actually tells you

Skills you can teach. The qualities above are hiring keys.

The candidate's side of fairness

A trial is a two-way audition. The other party is judging you the same way. So give the trial structure that would impress you:

  • A written mini-brief (that posting from Chapter 2 is half of it already).
  • Access only to what they need (a real one-off project brief, not your whole drive).
  • Answers within one business day.
  • Payment within a week of completion — even if the trial goes badly, you pay. A trial that ends in a no always still ends with a paid invoice.

The "no, thanks" message, done kindly

Trials produce polite nos. Send it warmly — this costs nothing and pays off in reputation:

Hi [name] — thanks so much for doing the trial edit; I paid you [amount]
already, but that's not why I'm writing. This isn't the fit I need for *this*
project (I need faster turnaround on volume), but you did careful work and I
want to flag that I'd happily recommend you to anyone needing [their actual
strength]. If another project DNA-matches that, I'll reach out. Thanks again
for your time and effort. — Dylan

That's a 60-second message; done right, it's also an open door.

Retainer vs. trial-based hire

Two useable shapes going in:

  1. Trial → part-time recurring (best if you have a task that recurs).
  2. Trial → specific project wrap, where the end date is already in view (best if the need is a burst rather than an ongoing one).

Both are legitimate. But be intentionally clear which one you're offering so the other person isn't discovering the boundary at the awkward moment.
