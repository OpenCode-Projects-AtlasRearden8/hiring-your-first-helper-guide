Chapter 8: Keeping Yourself from Drowning in Guidance

Education only, never legal or tax advice.

The drowning problem, named

The moment you Google "hiring a contractor small business," the internet flips into a firehose: 9 paragraphs of W-2 vs. 1099 nuance, 40-comment threads about misclassification edge cases, state-by-state tables, advice for companies with hundreds of employees mixed freely into advice for you.

And it's all marginally true, and almost none of it is about the decision actually on your desk. This chapter is a filter and a flashlight.

What the firehose assumes (and you don't have)

Most hiring guidance assumes you: run a payroll system, have an HR function, carry employment liability insurance, have a lawyer review agreements, and are comparing full-time benefit packages.

First-hire reality for most small businesses looks like: a person you found, a small recurring gig, an invoice you pay without drama. None of the scary articles address that situation, because it's rarely litigated — it's simply outside the domain they're writing for and also mostly fine.

The filter: is this article about my situation?

Before reading any hiring advice, ask the posting-first rule: would this guide's writer recognize your situation? Most rules you'll encounter across professional life apply to firms of 20–200 people, not to a solo author hiring a transcript helper at 4 hrs/week.

Concretely the rule that I'll offer:

  • Requirements for hiring employees: heavier, see a professional once, then ignore until relevancy changes.
  • Requirements for contractors under $600/year: honestly negligible; keep invoices and move on.
  • Requirements for contractors over $600/year: W-9 on file and track totals (Chapter 4 + 5 cover everything that matters at this scale).
  • Nothing in any article replaces a professional when the stakes rise — real money, incorporating, a real payroll, a genuine dispute.

Everything outside those four bands is mostly content, not law. Skim lightly, take what's usable, move on.

Panic-adjacent errors (the "one thing at a time" rule)

When overwhelmed by advice, beginners paradoxically make errors out of fear — maximizing compliance theater while missing the true basics. What you must not do:

  • Panic reading: ten hours of compliance articles instead of five minutes with your state's actual business resource page (most have them! Clear and free!) or one 20-minute call with an accountant.
  • Over-correcting: turning a $75 gig into a "just in case" letter-drafted contract marathon. Proportionality: pay, record, write plain agreements.
  • Doing nothing: the actual worst error. Most tasks you're dreading are 15-minute tasks wearing a costume.

And the positive version — the one, sufficient, boring, reliable move:

  1. Pay on time.
  2. Record what you paid.
  3. Put agreements in writing (plain language is fine).
  4. Take any classification question ($600+/year territory, employment vs. contractor doubt) to an actual professional, once, and stop re-litigating.

Everything else — newsletters, webinars, The Ultimate Guide To HR — is skippable until relevance arrives.

The name for the real problem

The real problem at your scale is rarely formal compliance. It's that you are one person who now has two jobs: your work, and being someone's boss. What trips small founders isn't usually the paperwork; it's:

  • forgetting to reply for a week and feeling guilty;
  • being afraid to give feedback;
  • letting scope creep silently grow ("while you're in there…");
  • not asking for help from your own networks because "I should manage this properly."

None of those have a law about them. They have habits about them.

One final breathing rule

When the guidance flood makes your chest tight:

Be clear, pay on time, write things down, say thank you.

Everything in this manual is an elaboration of those four. Do those, and you're already behaving more professionally than the median American small business. The rest is practice.
