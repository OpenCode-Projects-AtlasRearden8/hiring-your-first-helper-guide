# Chapter 7: Offboarding Kindly (and Cleaning Up Access)

*Education only, never legal or tax advice.*

## Two separations, never mixed up

There are two distinct events when work ends:

1. **The relationship end** — the goodbye. Emotional, conversational, mutual
   respect territory.
2. **The access end** — which tools they can still open. Cold, logistical,
   same-day.

Keeping these mixed up is how you get either cold procedure too soon, or —
much worse — **a former helper still holding active access to your email,
shared drive, or social accounts months later.** The second event needs a
checklist precisely because it isn't emotional. This chapter gives you both.

## Ending the work itself, with kindness

The friendly version:

- **If you're the one ending it:** say it directly and quickly — within a
  message or a call, never by fading out. "This isn't the fit anymore" plus a
  genuine specific thing they did well. People endlessly forgive a clear no;
  they rarely forgive silence.
- **Give notice they can act on.** Your working agreement said two weeks.
  That gives both sides a runway to wrap things in a dignified rather than
  abrupt way. (They may still be a reference, a future collaborator, or the
  person who tells their nurse friends what you're like to work with.)
- **Pay everything owed, immediately.** Final invoice, nothing withheld, on
  time. Final payment happens at the end of the relationship, on *schedule*,
  as a matter of course. Nothing else in this chapter works if this one
  doesn't.
- **End explicitly.** "Our work together wraps Friday" — not a fade into
  silence where your helper wonders if they're still employed. An explicit
  ending *is* kindness.

## The tool-access checklist (do this the same day)

Before the ending conversation, write down everything they currently have
access to. Then after the last day, revoke it all:

- [ ] Shared drives / folders (remove their access but **keep the files**)
- [ ] Email forwarding or filters you'd set up for them
- [ ] Any tool logins (scheduler, social accounts, editing software, AI
      subscriptions)
- [ ] Passwords they ever received — **rotate them** if they weren't per-person
      credentials
- [ ] Calendar invites / recurring meetings (cancel)
- [ ] Mailing lists, team channels, group chats
- [ ] Your domain/website/DNS registrar — only if they were helping there
      (double-check)
- [ ] Payment info: nothing of yours stored on their devices

Two general rules that avoid most disasters:

1. **Never share your own master password.** Give per-tool access; when a
   person leaves, revoke per-tool access. If you had to share one credential
   anyway, changing it is fast and harmless.
2. **Offboarding happens while they still have a helpful attitude.** Ask *them*
   for anything only they know (their list of logins, their mental process),
   while you can still just ask.

## The handback conversation (also very short)

After the goodbye, one low-key message serves fine:

> Also, housekeeping: can you copy anything project-related that only lives on
> your machine into our shared folder before Friday? And let me know if you've
> got any of my logins on any of your devices, so I can note which passwords
> I'll reset. Thanks!

Mostly people will do all of that anyway, and asking costs you nothing.

## If the ending wasn't smooth (the harder version)

Sometimes work ends badly — someone disappears, or the fit turned sour. Then
the access cleanup becomes urgent and the goodbye becomes brief:

- revoke access the same hour, no notice;
- rotate shared passwords same day;
- don't send emotional messages in writing — keep it factual only;
- pay what you owe (final invoice) even when you're unhappy with the ending;
  it closes the loop cleanly and keeps you the classy one;
- write a 3-line neutral note in your person folder: date ended, why (in one
  sentence, no extended essay), access revoked — so in six months you
  remember *your* side of it.

(If a person genuinely violates your confidentiality or does something illegal,
there are legal avenues — that's what professionals are for, and it's beyond
this guide's scope.)

## The goodbye, when possible, done warmly

One paragraph to send when an ending was genuinely mutual:

> Beth — thank you. The transcripts have never been tidier and the podcast
> recaps made a real difference. If your next thing needs a reference, I'll
> write one gladly. Good luck with [whatever's next] — I mean it.

Sixty seconds of warmth, and it's something that defuses the awkwardness of
goodbyes for both of you. Be generous — it costs nothing and it sets the tone
for how the whole working world can treat part-time helpers.
