# Chapter 6: Onboarding Without Info-Flooding

*Education only, never legal or tax advice.*

## The problem with dumping the manual

You care about this work and you want your helper to succeed, so you ship them
40 pages of context, 12 logins, your brand-voice essay, and a Slack welcome on
day one.

They nod enthusiastically and absorb roughly 8%.

That's not their failure. That's the failure mode of **onboarding-by-flood**:
it flatters how much you know and it helps nobody. Real onboarding is a
*staged drip* — exactly what they need, exactly when they need it.

## The three-stage rhythm

### Stage 1 — Day one: the minimum to not feel lost

Send exactly four things:

1. **Where the work lives** (one shared folder link; Chapter 5's one-liner).
2. **The one thing to do first** — *one*, small, low-stakes, achievable in
   under an hour (e.g., "clean the transcript for episode 12 using the
   prototype in the folder").
3. **How to ask you a question** (e.g., "Reply here, or Slack me; I answer
   within one business day; don't sit stuck longer than 20 minutes — ask").
4. **Your availability** honestly: "I'm most responsive in the mornings; I do
   not expect replies from you on weekends."

Deliberately *not* sent day one: brand guides, tool-by-tool training, the
"future project ideas" list, context for what *you* do the rest of the week.

### Stage 2 — Week one: training *only where they'll stumble*

 Run a **short** 30-minute working video call early in week one. Do not
teach everything — teach only:

- the thing they'll touch daily,
- the one or two things genuinely confusing without a demo,
- how to mark their own uncertainty instead of guessing ("if you're not sure,
  mark it `[?]` and move on" — this works stupendously well).

Then give **the first-week onboarding sheet** (in the extras at the back of
this guide) a once-over together: it's short. It has Monday through Friday,
one primary task, one bit of context, and one feedback moment per day.
They can print it or copy it.

### Stage 3 — Weeks 2–4: grow the perimeter slowly

Add things *as they become relevant*, a kitchen at a time:

- week 2: the next recurring task;
- week 3: a bit of autonomy ("you don't need to ask me before renaming files;
  just follow the naming system");
- week 4: a retro question ("What's one thing you wished you'd understood in
  week one?") — which also fixes your onboarding for next time.

## Day-one message (copy-and-modify template)

```
Subject: Welcome aboard — day one plan

Hi Beth,

Thrilled to have you. First: the work lives in this shared folder — [link].
That's home base; everything we make together, both of us, lives there.

Your first task (should take under an hour): clean the transcript for
episode 12. The rough version and a "how-to" note are inside the episode-12
folder. Don't aim for pretty; aim for *doneness* — you'll get the hang of my
quirks by doing, not by reading.

One rule: if you're stuck more than 20 minutes, message me. I answer within
one business day, usually faster in the mornings. It's genuinely no bother.

This week, small and steady: I'll send the next piece of work once I've seen
how you like this one. No homework beyond it. Welcome, for real.
— Dylan
```

That email weighs almost nothing but it does four jobs: home base, first
task, the ask-me rule, no-pressure-close. Everything else arrives later.

## Anti-flood principles, summarized

1. **Need-to-now over need-to-know.** Send information at the moment it
   becomes useful.
2. **One first task, small and real.** A win in hour one changes everything.
3. **Teach the check-in motion, not the encyclopedia.** The rule "ask after 20
   minutes stuck" matters more than any doc.
4. **Ritual beats documentation.** A short weekly reply-to ("Monday: reply
   here with what you're doing this week") does more for coordination than
   the best wiki.
5. **Fix the doc, not the conversation.** When they ask a question, answer it
   — *and* (this is the magic) update the place where they'd have looked, so
   next person won't need to ask. Your help slowly writes itself.

## What friendly accountability looks like

For a part-timer, the ideal check-in rhythm is one 10-minute weekly
exchange: they write what they did, what was tricky, and what's next; you
reply with "great / here's the one thing to adjust." Pre-talked, uniform,
tiny — and if a week goes by without them sending it, that's a chat for that
day, not a silent slide into "have they forgotten me?" Prickly silence is
where small team morale quietly goes to die.
