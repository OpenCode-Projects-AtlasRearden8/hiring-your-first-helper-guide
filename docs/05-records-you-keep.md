# Chapter 5: The Records You Should Keep

*Education only, never legal or tax advice.*

## The principle: boring beats thorough

You do not need a filing system. You need **three things**, in one place:

1. **The paper trail** of hiring someone (one folder).
2. **A payment log** (a running list, one file).
3. **One shared place** where the actual work lives.

That's it. Resist the urge to build systems. The whole point of hiring help is
to *remove* work from your life, not add a second job of "helper records
curator."

## 1. The person folder

One folder per person, e.g. `Beth Smith - podcast transcripts 2026`. Inside:

- the **posting** you hired from (a screenshot or paste is fine);
- their **W-9** (page one is everything);
- any **written agreement** (see below);
- their **invoice PDFs**, as they arrive;
- proof of **payment** (bank confirmation or processor receipt).

Two additional notes:

- Names/files shouldn't contain Social Security Numbers or images of IDs — the
  filled W-9 already contains what you need. Keep nothing extra sensitive in
  drive-synced spaces.
- Anything you *wouldn't want them reading* doesn't belong with the person
  folder; use judgment (and if a thought is uncomfortable, that's a sign to
  talk with the person, not to store it).

## 2. The payment log

A spreadsheet, once a month, five columns — **intentionally housebound:**

| Date paid | Person | Amount | Invoice # | How paid |

That's all. Your accountant can already run with that.

## About the written agreement

Do you need a lawyer-drafted contract for a 4 hr/week gig? Realistically no —
but you *do* need **something in writing**, and here's the plain-language
version that covers 95% of what causes small-business disputes:

```
**Working agreement: [name] — [task], starting [date]**

Rate: $XX/hr, billed weekly, 5 hr/week cap unless we both agree to extend.
We are independent parties: you send me invoices; I don't withhold taxes;
you pay your own taxes. I don't provide benefits, paid time off, or an
employment relationship.
Timeline: I expect this to continue indefinitely; either of us can end it
with [2 weeks] notice and a conversation, or immediately if something's
seriously wrong.
Ownership: everything you make for me I get full rights to use; any of my
existing materials I lend you remain mine and shouldn't be reused elsewhere.
Confidentiality: not a corporate veil; a promise not to embarrass one another
by sharing private business details or customer info. (I'll match the favor.)
Tools: you'll use [list]. When work ends, you'll delete any copies of my
files from your machine.
Signed: ______ / ______          Date: ______
```

That's not legal advice (a lawyer would want different wording); it's **the
written version of "we both know what we agreed"** — which is what a contract
is for at the small-business scale.

One extra note: agreements about *when you can end the work* matter more than
agreements about *what to do if it's great*. Include an ending clause.

## 3. The work lives somewhere sane

One shared folder or drive, and **one instruction**:

> "Everything we make together lives here in this one folder. If you ever can't
> find something, don't go hunting — it's in here somewhere, and if it's
> *really* not here, ask me and I'll go look."

That single sentence prevents 90% of first-week file chaos.

## The "audit myself" quarterly habit

Four times a year, one Sunday, 20 minutes:

- [ ] Every invoice paid?
- [ ] Payment log current?
- [ ] W-9 received for every helper? (Year-end 1099 prep: you're so glad.)
- [ ] Running total paid per person this year? ($600 threshold worth knowing.)
- [ ] Any outstanding access I should clean up? (Chapter 7 does it when
      someone leaves; you do a health check quarterly.)

Twenty-minute list. Do it and ten things become painless.
