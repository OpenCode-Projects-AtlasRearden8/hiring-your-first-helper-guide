# Chapter 1: Should You Hire? (And What Kind of Help?)

*Education only, never legal or tax advice.*

## The honest question behind the question

Before "who should I hire," the real question is: **what is the one recurring
task that reliably eats your best hours?**

Not the task that's annoying once. The one that shows up *every week* and makes
you dread opening your laptop. That's the hire-able shape of work.

## The five kinds of "help" (pick exactly one to start)

| Shape | What it looks like | Good first hire? |
|---|---|---|
| **One-off project** | "Build my site," "design the cover" | Great — lowest commitment |
| **Recurring part-time gig** | "4 hrs/week, sort transcripts" | Great — a real relationship grows |
| **A burst of seasonal help** | "November launch crunch" | Fine, but plan the ending up front |
| **Full employee** | Set schedule, benefits, withholding | Usually not your *first* move |
| **AI + automation instead** | "Can a robot just do this?" | Check this *before* hiring a human |

Full-time employees are a different, heavier category (payroll withholding,
benefits, tax stakes, and — this matters — how the law classifies the person).
If a full-time position is genuinely your answer, this guide still helps with
the *human* parts (posting, interviews, onboarding, offboarding), but take the
payroll/tax chapters to an accountant before you act.

## The AI-and-automation question first

Dylan's whole thesis is that a lot of "I need to hire someone" is actually
"I need this task to stop existing." Before humans:

- Is the task *repetitive and rule-based*? (sorting, transcribing, formatting)
  → try automation or an AI workflow first.
- Is the task *judgment-based*? (writing in your voice, deciding, creating)
  → a human helper still wins, and you can hand them a semi-automated
  workflow to use.

The honest ordering: **eliminate it → automate it → delegate it.** Hiring is
the third step for a reason.

## Contractor vs. employee, in plain English

This is legally loaded territory, so here's the *shape* of it, not the letter:

- **Contractor** runs their own business, controls *how* and *when* they work,
  uses their own tools, serves multiple clients, and sends you invoices.
- **Employee** works *for* you on *your* schedule, under your direction, with
  your equipment — and the law expects you to withhold taxes, carry
  unemployment coverage, and follow more rules.

The mistake that gets small businesses in trouble is calling someone a
"contractor" while treating them like an employee (fixed schedule, exclusive,
you direct every step, you supply everything). The test isn't the label on the
invoice; it's the working relationship. **When in doubt, ask an accountant or
attorney before you sign anything** — retrofitting a misclassified person later
truly is painful.

That's why this guide's sweet spot is: *small, well-scoped projects and
recurring gigs with clearly independent people.*

## Your first-hire decision, compressed

Answer these four on paper in ten minutes:

1. **The task:** one recurring task that eats my best hours is ______.
2. **The automation check:** I have/haven't tried automating it. Result: ______.
3. **The shape:** project / part-time gig / seasonal / (employee = see a pro).
4. **The number I can afford per month without flinching:** $______.

If you can't answer #4 honestly — pause, don't hire. Unaffordable hires fail
for money Reasons and it damages the relationship and your reputation.
