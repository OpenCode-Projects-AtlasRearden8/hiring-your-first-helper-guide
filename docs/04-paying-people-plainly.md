# Chapter 4: Paying People Plainly

*Education only, never legal or tax advice. Tax specifics (forms, deadlines,
withholding, deductibility) vary and change — this chapter explains the shape
of things so your accountant conversation takes ten minutes, not ten hours.*

## Plain beats clever

Payment isn't a mystery to solve; it's a promise to keep. Plain looks like:

- an agreed rate, in writing, before work starts;
- an invoice from them, a payment from you, both timestamped and traceable;
- a payment date that *always* happens, with no renegotiating.

That's most of it. Everything else is detail.

## Rate vs. flat fee — when each fits

| Structure | Fits when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Hourly** | Work varies in size; scope fuzzy | Ambiguity breeds anxiety on both sides. Ask for honest hour tracking |
| **Flat fee per project** | Scope is crystal clear | Scope creep — they do *more* work than paid for |
| **Flat monthly retainer** | Recurring, similar volume each month | Agree up front what "inside" the retainer is |
| **Per-unit** (per transcript, per episode) | Work scales in clean units | Units should be easy to count, no judgment calls |

Pick the least weird one for the work. For first hires, **hourly with a
cap** ("I'm budgeting ~5 hrs/week; flag me before you exceed it") is often the
friendliest: it protects you from runaway totals and protects them from
underestimated scope.

## The paperwork, in plain language

The contractor side of US small-business hiring, in one breath:

- **W-9:** a one-page form the contractor fills out *for you* (name, address,
  tax ID type). It's what you need before paying them. No withholding happens
  here — you're not their employer for tax purposes if they're genuinely
  independent.
- **Invoice:** the contractor sends you one per pay period (a simple template
  works fine). You pay it, and you *keep* it. That invoice is your proof that
  you paid.
- **1099-NEC:** if you pay a contractor $600+ over the year, you generally
  report that to the IRS at year-end. Your accountant will confirm your
  specifics; your job in the meantime is just to (a) collect W-9s on day one
  and (b) track totals. Chapter 5's log makes that effortless.
- **No withholding, no unemployment filing** (this is what "independent
  contractor" usually means in practice) — *but* only when the working
  relationship really is independent (Chapter 1). Don't retrofit; start right.

If instead you're hiring a *true employee*, stop and involve a payroll
service or accountant — that's a different, more regulated path and worth doing
right rather than improvising.

## How payment actually happens

Options, friendliest first for a tiny business:

- **Check-by-hand or ACH push** through your bank (slow but fine).
- **A payment processor** (Stripe, PayPal, etc.) — simple, small fee, and both
  sides get a clean timestamped record. Note: platforms have their own tax
  paperwork flows; read once, then forget.
- **Local bank transfer** if you're both American and your bank makes it easy.

Whatever you do: **pay them and write down that you paid.** Method matters
less than traceability.

## The rule that matters more than the paperwork

**Pay people on the day you said you'd pay them.** Every time. Even when
you're busy. Even when some earlier invoice felt imperfect.

Why this is the whole chapter in one sentence: cash-flow delays hurt part-time
people *disproportionately* more than they hurt a company, and one late
payment can make someone feel taken advantage of. Meanwhile paying *early* has
a bizarre effect: it makes people go the extra mile without being asked.
Pay on time, every time, and you've already outperformed most of the business
world.

## A tiny worked example

Dylan hires a helper to clean 4 podcast transcripts/week at $20/hr, capped at
5 hrs. He tells her: "Invoice me by Friday each week; I'll pay by the following
Tuesday, no exceptions."

Running log he keeps, updated as things happen:

| Date | What | Amount | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oct 1 | Helper picked; real rate $20/hr, 5 hr/week cap confirmed in writing | — | — |
| Oct 1 | W-9 received (before first invoice, thank you) | — | — |
| Oct 3 | Invoice #1 arrives (4.5 hrs) | $90 | Paid Oct 7 |
| Oct 10 | Invoice #2 arrives (5 hrs) | $100 | Paid Oct 14 |
| Oct 17 | Invoice #3 arrives (5 hrs) | $100 | Paid Oct 21 |
| Running total for year | paid-to-date | $290 | Fine; on track for $600 by year-end |

He's set. On Jan 1, handing his accountant a clean invoice log and W-9 copies
takes ten minutes, and 1099-NEC prep is simple math rather than detective work.
